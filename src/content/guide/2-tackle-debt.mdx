# Tackle Debt

Not all debt is created equal. A home loan on an appreciating house is fundamentally different from a 24% credit card balance. Before you invest aggressively, you need to deal with the expensive stuff.

**The simple rule:** if the interest rate on your debt is higher than what you'd reasonably expect to earn by investing (~7-8% a year, a common long-run assumption for shares (as at Oct 2026)), pay it off first. That's a guaranteed return.

## Good Debt vs Expensive Debt

| Type                   | Example                                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Low-cost / "good" debt | Home loan                                  |
| Medium-cost debt       | Some car loans                             |
| Expensive / "bad" debt | Credit cards, payday loans, personal loans |

Compare the interest rate on each debt with the rule above. Rates change with the cash rate, so check what you're actually paying.

Interest on the home you live in is **not tax deductible** in Australia or New Zealand. Only the part of a loan used to earn income, such as a rental property, can be.

**Expensive debt is a financial emergency.** A credit card at, for example, 24% p.a. means every $1,000 you carry costs you $240 a year in interest. No investment reliably returns 24%. Paying off that card _is_ the best investment you can make.

## The Order: Cash Cushion First

Before you throw every spare dollar at debt:

1. **Build Tier 1 of your cash cushion first** — at least 1 month of expenses in your everyday account.
2. **Keep making minimum payments on everything** — never miss a minimum payment.
3. **Then attack the debt** — with any money above minimums, use one of the strategies below.

## Two Strategies: Avalanche vs Snowball

There are two well-known approaches to paying off multiple debts. Both work. The "best" one is whichever you'll actually stick with.

### Avalanche Method (Lowest Total Cost)

Pay minimums on everything, then put all extra money towards the debt with the **highest interest rate**. When that's paid off, roll the payment to the next highest rate.

**Best for:** People who are motivated by maths and logic.

### Snowball Method (Fastest Wins)

Pay minimums on everything, then put all extra money towards the debt with the **smallest balance**. When that's paid off, roll the payment to the next smallest balance.

**Best for:** People who need visible progress to stay motivated.

## When to Pay Debt vs Invest

| Debt interest rate | What to do                                                                   |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Above 8%**       | Pay it off before investing (beyond your employer super or default KiwiSaver contributions) |
| **5-8%**           | Judgment call — consider splitting extra money 50/50                         |
| **Below 5%**       | Generally fine to invest while making payments                               |

**In Australia**, your employer's Super Guarantee is paid regardless, but it's usually better to clear expensive debt before you salary sacrifice extra into super. **In New Zealand, keep contributing to KiwiSaver at least at the default rate**, regardless of debt. Your employer's contribution and the government's 25c per $1 (from 1 Jul 2025) are an instant return that no debt costs.

<Callout type="tip" title="The hybrid approach">
  If you have debt in the 5-8% grey zone and can't decide, split your extra money: half towards the
  debt, half towards investing. This is "good enough" — and good enough beats indecision.
</Callout>

## Your Debt-to-Income Ratio

In Australia and New Zealand, **debt-to-income (DTI)** usually means your total debt as a multiple of your gross yearly income:

**DTI = Total debt ÷ Gross yearly income**

For example, $540,000 of debt on a $90,000 income is a DTI of 6. Regulators limit how much lending banks can do at high DTIs:

| Country         | Limit on banks                                                                                              |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Australia**   | At most 20% of new home lending at a DTI of 6 or more (from 1 Feb 2026, [APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/activating-debt-income-limits-macroprudential-policy-tool)). Banks also test that you could afford repayments at 3 percentage points above your loan rate (as at Oct 2026) |
| **New Zealand** | At most 20% of owner-occupier lending at a DTI over 6, and 20% of investor lending at a DTI over 7 (from 1 Jul 2024, [RBNZ](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/news-and-events/news/2024/05/reserve-bank-activates-debt-to-income-restrictions)) |

A high DTI doesn't automatically mean you'll be refused, but it leaves less room if rates rise or your income falls.

<Callout type="warning">

- **Salary sacrificing extra into super while carrying expensive debt** — in Australia, the tax saving won't beat a 20% credit card. Clear the card first.
- **Cutting KiwiSaver below the default rate to pay off low-rate debt** — you'd give up your employer's contribution and the government contribution, which beat paying off a 5% loan every time.
- **Consolidating debt into a longer term** — a lower monthly payment at a lower rate but over 10 more years can mean _more_ total interest paid. Always compare total cost, not just the monthly number.
- **Paying off the home loan early when you have credit card debt** — always attack the highest rate first.
- **Using home equity to pay off credit cards, then running up the cards again** — you've converted unsecured debt to secured debt and didn't fix the spending pattern.
- **Not making a plan** — "I'll pay extra when I can" leads to inconsistent payments. Pick a method, set a specific monthly amount, and automate it.

</Callout>
